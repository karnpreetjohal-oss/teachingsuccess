How to answer A-Level Biology data-analysis questions

Practise A-Level Biology data analysis with an original results table, percentage-change calculation, model comparisons and an exam-answer checklist.

Read the guideA-Level Biology tuition

By Teaching Success · Published 3 November 2026 · 6 minute read

Start here: Start with the variables and units, identify what the question asks, then support your answer with selected data. Keep a description of the results separate from a biological explanation, and do not claim more than the investigation can show.

Knowing the topic is only part of answering an A-Level Biology data question. A student may understand plant nutrition yet lose the thread when asked to compare treatments, calculate a change or evaluate a conclusion. A useful response connects the instruction, the evidence and the relevant biological idea.

The practice dataset below is fictional and was created for this guide. It is not an exam-board question, a real study or a prediction of future exam content. Use your own board’s specification and mark schemes alongside this practice.

Read the instruction before writing an explanation

First identify what was changed, what was measured and how the results are expressed. Check whether the values are totals, means, percentages or rates. AQA’s A-Level Biology mathematical requirements include interpreting tables and graphs, using percentages and working with measures of variation.

  • Describe: state the pattern shown, including a useful value or change.
  • Compare: make direct comparisons between the conditions, with data where relevant.
  • Explain: connect the result to a biological mechanism.
  • Evaluate: weigh the evidence and limitations before judging a claim.

These are working prompts, not a replacement for the wording of a particular question. If the question asks for a calculation, show the calculation; a paragraph about the topic does not answer it.

An original results table to practise with

Imagine seedlings of the same species and starting age grown for seven days at four nitrate concentrations. Three separate seedlings are measured in each treatment. Other growing conditions are intended to be the same. The results below give final root length, not a directly measured rate of growth.

Before answering, explain each heading aloud. The concentration is in mmol dm−3; the measured length is in mm. The concentration steps are not all equal, so a graph with a numerical concentration axis must reflect the actual spacing.

Describe the pattern with selected numbers

Practice question: describe the effect of increasing nitrate concentration on mean root length

Example answer: Mean root length rises from 8 mm at 0 mmol dm−3 to 18 mm at 4 mmol dm−3. Increasing the concentration from 4 to 8 mmol dm−3 is associated with a much smaller increase, from 18 to 19 mm.

The answer identifies the overall trend and its change across the tested range. “It goes up” lacks the named measurement and evidence. “It is directly proportional” is also unsupported: doubling the concentration from 2 to 4 does not double the mean root length from 12 to 24 mm.

When comparing two treatments, put both in the same sentence. For example: “At 4 mmol dm−3, mean root length is 6 mm greater than at 2 mmol dm−3.” Add an explanation only if it is requested.

Calculate percentage change using the starting value

Calculate the percentage increase in mean root length between 2 and 4 mmol dm−3. The original mean is 12 mm and the new mean is 18 mm.

Percentage increase = (new − original) ÷ original × 100

(18 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = 50%

The 6 mm difference is half of the starting 12 mm, which gives a useful sense check. Dividing by 18 would use the wrong reference value for the increase from 12.

Your turn: calculate the increase from 8 mm to 12 mm.

Show the calculation

(12 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = 50%. The absolute increase is 4 mm; the relative increase is 50%. Those describe different aspects of the same change.

Evaluate a claim without overstating the evidence

Consider: “Increasing nitrate concentration always produces longer roots.” The means increase across these four treatments, but the data do not establish “always”. They cover one species, a limited range, one duration and three seedlings per treatment.

The 8 mmol dm−3 group also has a larger spread: lengths range from 16 to 22 mm, compared with 17 to 19 mm at 4 mmol dm−3. A higher mean alone does not establish a statistically significant difference. Overlapping ranges alone do not settle that question either; appropriate statistical analysis depends on the design, data and assumptions.

A useful improvement should address a specific limitation. For example, use more independent seedlings per treatment to estimate variation better, and check that temperature, light, water availability and the other solution components are controlled. Repeating measurements does not automatically remove systematic error.

AQA’s practical assessment guidance includes evaluating methods, identifying controlled variables and considering uncertainty. The point is to connect a limitation to its effect on the conclusion, rather than adding “repeat it” to every answer.

Keep the biological explanation tied to the question

If asked for a possible explanation, nitrate supplies nitrogen that plants can use to make amino acids and proteins needed for growth. That offers a biological link between nutrient supply and the measured outcome. The table alone does not demonstrate the cellular mechanism or explain every difference between individual seedlings.

Also distinguish final length from growth. To compare how much each root grew, initial lengths would be useful. To investigate changing growth rates over time, measurements at several times would be needed. Naming the variable correctly prevents an answer from claiming a measurement that was never made.

A checklist before moving to the next question

  1. Have I answered the instruction rather than everything I know about the topic?
  2. Have I named the variables and included the correct units?
  3. Have I used relevant values and compared like with like?
  4. Is the calculation based on the correct starting value, with working shown?
  5. Have I separated a pattern, an explanation and a judgement about evidence?
  6. Have I avoided claiming causation, significance or certainty beyond what the question supports?

Keep one marked data question for review and identify which of these steps failed first. For practical-method preparation, use our A-Level Biology practical checklist alongside the work set by your school or college.
