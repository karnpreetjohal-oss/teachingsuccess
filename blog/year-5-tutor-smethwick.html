What a Year 5 tutor should really be helping with

Year 5 support should focus on upper-KS2 confidence, longer homework routines and preparation for a bigger academic jump. At this stage, the aim is usually to make school feel steadier week by week rather than to pile on extra work without a plan.

Where Year 5 pupils usually start to wobble

The common pressure point in Year 5 is when families are balancing normal school progress with possible SATs or 11+ interest ahead. A strong plan makes those problems visible early, then works through them calmly before they grow into a bigger Key Stage 2 issue.

What the first half-term of Year 5 tuition should include

  • Short checks on inference, richer vocabulary and more organised written explanations so the tutor can see what is secure and what still needs direct teaching
  • Regular work on fractions, percentages, problem-solving and upper-KS2 reasoning stamina rather than waiting until the next school test exposes the gap again
  • A simple weekly routine that parents and pupils can actually maintain
  • Enough review to build confidence, not just enough to finish the worksheet

Practical guide · Updated 23 September 2026

Choose which work deserves a second attempt

Redoing everything can make practice feel endless. Year 5 pupils can begin learning how to select one piece of work where a second attempt has a clear purpose.

A practical example

Place three recent tasks side by side: one completed independently, one solved after a useful hint and one not yet understood. Choose the middle task. Ask what the hint revealed, then try a similar question with the hint hidden. This tests whether the support can now be reduced.

Save the difficult task for a teacher’s explanation rather than repeating a failed method alone. Leave the already-secure task unless a later check suggests otherwise. A short review can therefore produce three different decisions—maintain, practise or ask for teaching—instead of treating every marked page as another full homework assignment.

Build a vocabulary card that can be used, not just recited

Choose one word from current schoolwork. Record a child-friendly meaning, a sentence that uses it correctly and a near-example that does not fit. For “estimate”, distinguish an informed approximate calculation from an unsupported guess.

On another day, give a new situation and ask whether the word applies. If the child remembers the definition but cannot use it, revisit the examples. Keep subject meanings explicit: a “table” in maths or science is not necessarily furniture. A few usable words are more valuable than a large copied list.
