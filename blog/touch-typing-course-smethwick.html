Why touch typing is worth teaching properly

Typing is a daily skill for schoolwork, coursework, applications and office tasks. Learners who search for every key have to divide their attention between the keyboard and the sentence they are trying to write. A structured course replaces that habit gradually, with accuracy established before speed.

The programme is designed and led by a qualified teacher with an MEd in Educational Technology and Learning Design, so practice is sequenced in small steps and reviewed using evidence rather than guesswork.

How the course is structured

  • Stage 1 — posture, home row and finger discipline; accuracy over speed from the first session
  • Stage 2 — expand across the alphabet, common words and short sentences without looking down
  • Stage 3 — add capitals, numbers and punctuation once the core key positions are secure
  • Stage 4 — practise real tasks such as school paragraphs, emails and documents at a sustainable pace

Short daily practice between sessions (10–15 minutes) is set and reviewed each week, so progress is measured rather than hoped for.

For school-age children and students

More fluent typing can reduce the effort spent finding keys during homework and longer written tasks. For a student who already uses a laptop as their normal way of working, accurate typing helps them use that tool more effectively.

Typing lessons do not create an entitlement to exam access arrangements. Schools and exam centres decide whether a word processor is appropriate under current rules, based on evidence and the learner's normal way of working.

For adults

Adults often book because typing slows down email, reports, applications or data entry. The adult course uses the same accuracy-first structure but applies it to workplace text sooner, often alongside Microsoft Office training for a broader workplace-skills plan.

Late August 2026: Build a Ten-Minute Typing Routine That Lasts

Touch typing improves through frequent accurate practice, not occasional long sessions. Before school or work routines restart, establish correct finger placement, screen posture and a short daily block that can survive a busy week.

  • Measure a calm baseline: Record accuracy and words per minute without encouraging frantic speed.
  • Hide the hands gradually: Use a light cover only when key positions are familiar enough to avoid frustration.
  • Practise difficult pairs: Create short drills for recurring letter combinations rather than repeating easy text.
  • Retest after two weeks: Compare accuracy first, then speed, using a passage of similar difficulty.

How Touch Typing Progress Is Measured

A speed score on its own can reward rushed, inaccurate typing. The course records three measures: accuracy, words per minute and how often the learner looks down. A short unfamiliar passage is repeated at sensible intervals so the comparison is fair.

  • Accuracy first: repeated errors are identified by key or finger instead of hidden inside an overall score.
  • Comfort: posture, hand position and unnecessary tension are checked before increasing practice time.
  • Transfer: the learner types a school, study or workplace paragraph rather than completing drills only.

Touch Typing Course FAQs

What age can children start a touch typing course?
Readiness matters more than a fixed age. A child should be able to reach the home-row keys comfortably, follow short instructions and practise for a few accurate minutes.

How long does it take to learn to touch type?
The time varies with starting habits and practice. Progress is measured through accuracy, words per minute and the ability to type unfamiliar text without looking down.

Can touch typing help children with dyslexia or handwriting difficulty?
Typing may give some learners another way to record ideas, but it is not a diagnosis or exam-access assessment. Schools and exam centres decide access arrangements.

Do adults take touch typing courses too?
Yes. Adult lessons use email, reports, applications and other realistic workplace text while building accurate keyboard habits.
