What a Year 4 tutor should really be helping with

Year 4 support should focus on building independence, stronger habits and more consistent accuracy across the week. At this stage, the aim is usually to make school feel steadier week by week rather than to pile on extra work without a plan.

Where Year 4 pupils usually start to wobble

The common pressure point in Year 4 is when a pupil can start work but struggles to sustain focus through longer tasks. A strong plan makes those problems visible early, then works through them calmly before they grow into a bigger Key Stage 2 issue.

What the first half-term of Year 4 tuition should include

  • Short checks on comprehension evidence, paragraph shape and more controlled grammar so the tutor can see what is secure and what still needs direct teaching
  • Regular work on multiplication fluency, fractions, perimeter and multi-step reasoning rather than waiting until the next school test exposes the gap again
  • A simple weekly routine that parents and pupils can actually maintain
  • Enough review to build confidence, not just enough to finish the worksheet

Why this Year 4 page is different

Parents searching specifically for a Year 4 tutor are usually looking for more independent written work, stronger multiplication recall and the bridge into upper-KS2 expectations. Use the current class topics and teacher feedback to decide which of these skills needs attention first.

Practical guide · Updated 23 September 2026

A two-minute explanation without the worksheet

Completing a page with support does not tell you which part a pupil can explain alone. A brief verbal handover gives a different view of what has become secure.

A practical example

After an ordinary task, put the worksheet face down. Ask the child to explain one idea to a toy, sibling or adult: why a character acted a certain way, how a times-table fact was found, or what made a sentence clearer. Invite a small drawing if that helps.

Ask one genuine follow-up question rather than correcting every detail immediately. If the explanation breaks down, return to the exact example and rebuild that part together. Record the idea, not a score. Revisit it in a different context later so confidence rests on understanding rather than remembering the wording of the page.

Make a self-check that is smaller than the task

Before a short piece of writing, agree two things the child will check afterwards, such as whether each sentence begins clearly and whether the reader can identify who is speaking. Keep the checklist beside the task.

Ask the pupil to point to evidence for each check rather than tick both immediately. Do not expand the list to every possible spelling and punctuation issue. A manageable check builds a habit of reviewing work; an exhaustive list can turn a small task into a second, harder assignment.
