11+ vocabulary practice: a 15-minute routine with examples

Try a manageable 11+ vocabulary routine for Years 4 and 5, with original word-in-context questions, answers and a simple review method for parents.

Read the guide11+ tuition

By Teaching Success · Published 3 November 2026 · 5 minute read

Start here: Choose a few useful words from reading, explain them in context, use them in a new sentence and revisit them later without showing the definition. The 15-minute routine below is a flexible starting point for Years 4 and 5, not a required daily quota.

A child may recognise a word on a flashcard but still be unsure what it means in a passage. Useful 11+ vocabulary practice moves between reading, explanation and using the word. Parents do not need a huge word list to begin: a short passage and two or three words are enough for one focused session.

The activities below are original Teaching Success practice examples, not questions from an entrance paper. They support vocabulary and reasoning but do not reproduce a particular school’s test.

Check the target test before choosing materials

11+ formats differ. GL Assessment explains that schools or admissions authorities choose the subjects and balance of question types. Check the target school’s current admissions information and intended entry year, then use appropriate familiarisation materials. See GL Assessment’s parent FAQs and its free familiarisation materials.

Vocabulary practice is one part of preparation. It should sit alongside reading, maths and any reasoning areas required by the actual test. The suggested timings here describe a home learning session, not entrance-test timings.

A flexible 15-minute vocabulary routine

TimeActivityA useful parent prompt
3 minutesRead a short passage together and choose two or three uncertain words.“What is happening around this word?”
4 minutesSuggest a meaning, then check a child-friendly dictionary.“Which part of the sentence supports your idea?”
4 minutesUse each word in a new sentence or contrast it with a near-synonym.“Would that word still fit if the situation changed?”
4 minutesTry a short question and revisit a word from an earlier session.“Can you explain it without looking at the definition?”

Shorten the session if the child is tired or the reading is demanding. The aim is a clearer understanding of a few words, not finishing a fixed number of cards. Fit the routine into a manageable Year 5 preparation plan with time for ordinary reading and rest.

Worked example: “reluctant” in context

Read: “Although the others hurried towards the dark tunnel, Amira was reluctant to leave the sunlight.”

Which meaning fits best? Unwilling, exhausted, delighted or invisible?

Show the answer and explanation

Unwilling. The contrast with the others hurrying ahead suggests hesitation about entering. “Exhausted” describes being very tired, but the sentence gives no evidence of tiredness. “Delighted” points in the opposite direction.

Now ask for a different sentence: “Leo was reluctant to lend his new sketchbook.” The child should explain what Leo does not want to do. Then compare reluctant with unable: someone can be able to do something but unwilling to do it.

Context gives clues, but it will not always identify a precise meaning. Encourage a reasonable suggestion followed by checking, rather than treating every unfamiliar word as something that can be guessed with certainty.

Five words to explore beyond a definition

WordMeaning for this practiceTry this question
CautiousCareful because there may be a riskHow might a cautious cyclist approach a busy junction?
ScarceAvailable only in small amounts relative to needWhy might drinking water become scarce during a drought?
DiligentWorking carefully and with steady effortHow could a diligent pupil check an answer?
InferWork something out from evidenceWhat could muddy shoes suggest, and what do they not prove?
AmbiguousOpen to more than one interpretationWhy is “Meet me by the bank” unclear without more information?

Ask for a near-synonym, then test whether it fits the same sentence. “Cautious” and “frightened” can overlap in a situation, but they are not interchangeable. A cautious person may simply be taking sensible care.

Three quick questions to try

1. Closest meaning

“The diligent gardener checked the seedlings each morning.” Which word is closest to diligent: careless, conscientious, impatient or gloomy?

Show answer 1

Conscientious. Both words describe taking care and applying effort. The other options do not express that meaning.

2. Opposite meaning

Which word is closest to the opposite of scarce in “Fresh food was scarce”: plentiful, expensive, distant or delicate?

Show answer 2

Plentiful. Scarce concerns limited availability; plentiful concerns a large supply. Expensive food is not necessarily scarce.

3. Meaning changes with context

Compare “The seats were reserved for guests” with “He was reserved when meeting strangers.” Does reserved mean the same thing?

Show answer 3

No. The seats were set aside for particular people. The person was quiet or restrained in how he expressed himself. Reading the surrounding words helps identify the intended meaning.

Revisit words in a new setting

Keep a small notebook with the word, its original sentence, a plain explanation and a new example. On another day, cover the explanation and ask the child to use the word. Later, revisit it in a different passage or question.

Record “used independently”, “needed a clue” or “needs another explanation”. If a word is repeatedly uncertain, change the example or compare it with a confusing alternative. Copying the definition again may not reveal the misunderstanding.

Common parent questions

How many words should my child learn each week?

Use a small number they can explain and revisit. This guide does not set a universal word target. Increase or reduce the number in response to understanding, reading demands and the rest of their preparation.

Should every vocabulary session be timed?

No. Give new meanings time to become clear. When the child understands the task, use suitable practice to become familiar with the target test’s format and pace.
