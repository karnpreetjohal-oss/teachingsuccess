How to build a five-slide PowerPoint interview presentation

Plan a clear job-interview presentation with a five-slide structure, timed rehearsal and a practical checklist for questions and delivery.

PowerPoint training in Birmingham

By Teaching Success · Published 16 September 2026 · 3 minute read

An interview presentation is not a competition to use the most PowerPoint features. It is a short opportunity to show how you think, organise evidence and communicate a recommendation. Start with the employer's brief, especially the question, time limit, permitted materials and whether questions are included in the allotted time.

The five-slide structure below is an example for a ten-minute presentation, not a rule for every interview. If the employer provides a different format, follow it. Prepare a useful answer when the panel challenges an assumption

An interview presentation is not finished when the last slide looks polished. A panel may ask about a trade-off, an uncertain figure or what you would do differently with more time.

A practical example

For each major recommendation, write one assumption and one limitation. Ask someone to challenge one of them. Practise acknowledging the uncertainty, explaining the evidence you do have and identifying the next check you would make.

Avoid inventing a confident answer to fill silence. Keep fictional scenarios and illustrative figures clearly labelled, and follow the employer’s presentation instructions. A concise, honest explanation of the limits of your analysis can be more useful than adding extra slides that do not address the question.

Slide 1: answer the brief

Use a title that makes your topic specific. “Improving the new-customer handover” is clearer than “My presentation”. Add one sentence explaining the outcome you are proposing and briefly introduce how you will address the question.

For example, a fictional task might ask how you would reduce missed handovers in a small team. Your opening could propose a shared checklist with a named owner and a weekly review. Label any invented figures as assumptions. Do not present demonstration data as results from a real employer.

Slide 2: describe the problem and evidence

Explain what currently happens, where the difficulty appears and what evidence you would check. A simple process or three short observations is often enough. Make a distinction between something the brief states and something you would need to investigate.

If drawing on your own experience, remove confidential names, commercial details and identifying customer information. An interview is not permission to disclose another organisation's data. Choose an example you are authorised to discuss and focus on your contribution.

Slide 3: show your proposed approach

Present two or three practical steps. For the handover example, these might be agreeing required information, assigning responsibility and piloting the process with one team. Explain why you chose them and mention a realistic trade-off.

Put the core message on the slide and keep supporting detail in speaker notes. If you cannot explain a bullet without reading a paragraph, the point probably needs simplifying. A panel should be able to understand the structure while listening to you.

Slide 4: explain implementation and checks

Show who would do what, in what order, and how you would know whether the change helped. Distinguish the measure from the desired outcome: “percentage of complete handovers” is a measure; “a more reliable handover” is the intended outcome.

Avoid promising a percentage improvement without a basis. Instead, explain how you would establish a baseline and compare results fairly. Mention one plausible risk, such as staff not using the checklist, and the action you would take to investigate it.

Slide 5: finish with a clear recommendation

Restate the decision or next step. Do not end with a dense summary of every previous bullet. One recommendation and two reasons give the panel something concrete to question.

Prepare for questions such as “What would you do first?”, “What information is missing?” and “What if your proposal does not work?” Practise saying when you would seek advice rather than bluffing an answer.

Rehearse the time, including transitions

For a ten-minute limit, try approximately one minute for the opening, two minutes each for the three middle slides, and one minute for the conclusion. This leaves a small buffer; adapt it if questions are part of the limit.

Microsoft explains how to use Rehearse Timings. Speak aloud during rehearsal rather than clicking through silently. Check whether saved timings will advance slides automatically, and use manual advancement if that suits the interview.

Finally, test the presentation on the equipment or meeting platform you will use. Keep a PDF backup if the employer permits it and practise without animations. Use the presentation checklist to check readability, accessibility and file readiness before sending it.
