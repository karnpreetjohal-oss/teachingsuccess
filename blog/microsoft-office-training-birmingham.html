Which Microsoft Office course do you actually need?

Start with the task you need to complete. A job description asking for “Microsoft Office skills” might mean making a spreadsheet report, formatting a document or presenting an idea. Those need different practice.

  • Numbers, trackers or monthly reports: choose one-to-one Excel training. Bring an example of the calculation or report you need to understand.
  • Interview slides or a work presentation: choose PowerPoint training. Start with the audience, brief and time limit.
  • CVs, letters or long documents: ask about Word lessons covering styles, headings and a document that remains tidy when edited.
  • A task crossing several apps: discuss a combined plan, such as turning spreadsheet results into a short report and presentation.

Training is led by Kevin Johal, a qualified teacher with an MEd in Educational Technology and Learning Design. Tell us what you can already do and the software you use so the first conversation is specific to your needs.

What Microsoft Office training can cover

  • Word — professional documents, CVs and cover letters, formatting that behaves, templates, letters and reports
  • Excel — from first formulas through to lookups, charts and pivot tables (see the dedicated Excel training page)
  • PowerPoint — clear slides, design basics, presenting confidently, speaker notes and handouts
  • Outlook and everyday skills — email management, calendars, attachments, file organisation and cloud storage

Who these sessions are for

Three groups book most often: working adults who need sharper Office skills for their current role or a promotion; jobseekers and returners to work whose applications keep meeting "proficient in Microsoft Office" requirements; and older adults and beginners who want patient, unrushed teaching that starts from the beginning without embarrassment.

Students are welcome too — coursework, presentations and sixth-form study all lean on these tools earlier than most families expect.

Choose a Focused Course or a Combined Office Plan

A combined Microsoft Office plan is useful when a role requires you to move between documents, spreadsheets, presentations, email and file storage. Each session follows one realistic workflow so the applications are not learned as disconnected menus.

Choose the dedicated Excel training course for formulas, lookups, tables, pivot tables and dashboards. Choose the focused PowerPoint training course when the immediate goal is an interview presentation, workplace deck or study assignment.

Three useful projects to bring to a lesson

Training becomes more memorable when every tool solves a real problem. Bring one unfinished task and leave with both a stronger result and a repeatable method.

  • An interview presentation: Turn a loose brief into a five-slide story with a clear opening, evidence and recommendation.
  • A monthly spreadsheet: Clean inconsistent entries, add reliable formulas and create a chart that answers one management question.
  • A professional document: Build a CV, report or letter with styles, spacing and headings that remain consistent when the content changes.

How the training works

Keep one source of truth when a report crosses Office apps

A report becomes fragile when the same figure is typed separately into a spreadsheet, document and presentation. Plan where the original number lives and how each output will be checked.

A practical example

Use fictional monthly totals in Excel, write a short explanation in Word and present one comparison in PowerPoint. Change an input, then follow the update through all three outputs. Note whether a chart was pasted as a static picture or linked, because those choices behave differently.

Record the update process in a short handover note. Use a practice copy, not confidential workplace data, and confirm the software versions before choosing a workflow. The useful learning outcome is a report that can be updated consistently, not simply knowing where each application’s buttons are.

Microsoft: linked charts and pictures · Checked 19 September 2026

Make the handover explain where the numbers came from

For a fictional monthly report, keep a short note of the source workbook, reporting period, filters and date checked. Ask another person to trace one figure in the presentation back to its source without your verbal help.

If they cannot, improve the labels or handover note before adding more slides. Use made-up data for practice and avoid distributing private workplace files. The exercise tests whether a report can be understood and maintained, not just whether the author can move a chart between applications.

What Progress Looks Like

Useful progress is visible in completed work: a document that keeps its formatting, a spreadsheet that updates correctly, a presentation that is easy to follow, or an inbox routine that makes actions easier to find. At the end of each training block, repeat a task from the first session with less support and compare the result.
