Parents’ evening questions that lead to a useful learning plan

Six questions, a worked example and a follow-up sheet to turn school feedback into a manageable next step.

Use the follow-up sheet

By Teaching Success · Published 7 October 2026 · 4 minute read

“Needs more detail.” “Must check their work.” “Should contribute more.” These comments can start a useful conversation, but they do not yet tell a child what to do differently. At parents’ evening, aim to leave with an example, one priority and a way to check it. You do not need a long list of extra worksheets.

Before the meeting: ask your child first

Ask which lesson feels manageable, where they get stuck and what they would like you to ask. Bring one recent piece of work if available. Note the difference between a subject difficulty and a practical obstacle: not understanding fractions needs a different response from not being able to read the homework instructions.

Choose two or three questions from the list below if appointment time is short. Keep wider concerns about wellbeing, attendance or ongoing support separate from a quick subject target, and arrange the right follow-up conversation with school.

Six useful questions to ask

  1. What can my child now do independently? Ask for an example of a strength, not only a grade or a comparison with classmates.
  2. Could you show me where the next difficulty appears? A sentence, calculation or marked answer makes the feedback easier to understand.
  3. Which one skill should we prioritise? Separate the immediate next step from everything the pupil will eventually need to learn.
  4. What would a successful attempt look like? Ask the teacher to explain the feature to look for, rather than simply providing an answer to memorise.
  5. What small activity would fit at home? Check the method, materials and amount of help expected. Agree what to do if the pupil cannot start.
  6. How and when should we review it? Confirm the school's preferred contact route and a realistic review point; do not assume the teacher can mark extra work every day.

Worked example: from “more detail” to a clear task

The following is an invented reading example, not a pupil case study. A child answers “Maya is nervous” and receives the comment “add more detail”. Simply making the sentence longer may not address what is missing.

Try this short passage: Maya checked the door number twice. She smoothed her notes and waited for someone else to knock. Ask what Maya might be feeling and which words support that view. One possible answer is: “Maya may be nervous because she checks the number twice and waits for someone else to knock.” Other interpretations need evidence too.

The learning goal is now specific: connect an inference to a detail in the text. Next, use a different short passage and let the pupil attempt the same skill without copying the model. Check whether the chosen detail actually supports the idea. There is no need to count extra adjectives or demand a whole page of writing.

For maths, the same conversation might turn “check your answers” into a precise check: substitute the solution into the original equation. Ask the teacher which check fits the current topic; one checking method will not suit every question.

A follow-up sheet you can copy or print

Use your browser’s Print option to keep this guide, or copy these prompts into a notebook. Complete them with your child and use the teacher's advice to choose the task.

PromptWhat to record
A strengthOne thing I can do without someone stepping in.
My next skillOne action, such as linking a reading inference to evidence.
My small practice taskThe question or activity agreed with the teacher.
Help I usedWhat I completed alone and where I needed a prompt.
My next attemptA different example testing the same skill.
ReviewWhen we will look again and how we will share a concern.

After the meeting: review the attempt, not just completion

Start with the strength before discussing the priority. Ask your child to explain the task back in their own words. If they needed substantial help, record that rather than presenting an adult-assisted answer as independent progress.

Look again at the agreed review point. Can the child use the skill on a different example? If not, note the exact sticking point and ask what to adjust. For a maths-specific record, use our GCSE maths error log.

If extra tuition is being considered, bring the school feedback and an attempted task to the first conversation. Our questions to ask before choosing a tutor can help you compare support. Tuition should work alongside school learning; a single meeting or lesson cannot promise a particular grade.
