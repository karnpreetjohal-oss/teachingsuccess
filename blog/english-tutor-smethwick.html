Practical guide · Updated 23 September 2026

Make a sentence easier to follow by changing its order

A sentence can be grammatically possible but difficult for a reader to follow. Editing the order of information gives writing practice a purpose beyond correcting spelling.

A practical example

Compare “After finding the key that had fallen behind the chair, Amir opened the cupboard” with two shorter sentences that present the events in sequence. Neither length is automatically better; discuss which version suits the intended reader and pace.

Try the same edit on a pupil’s own paragraph. Read both versions aloud and ask another person to explain what happened. Keep the version that communicates the intended relationship most clearly, rather than assuming that longer sentences always represent more advanced writing.

Read a draft aloud to find a missing connection

Write three sentences explaining an everyday process, then read them to someone who has not seen the plan. Ask the listener to identify a point where an action, object or reason is missing.

For example, “Put it inside. Close the lid. Wait” leaves the object and purpose unclear. Rewrite the instructions with precise nouns and an appropriate sequence. Reading aloud is useful here because it reveals what the writer has assumed the reader already knows; it is not a substitute for checking the task’s requirements.
