Choosing a tutor in Smethwick: what to ask before booking

Compare one-to-one, small-group and online tuition in Smethwick. Use a practical first-lesson checklist to choose support around your child's needs.

Smethwick tuition options

By Teaching Success · Published 16 September 2026 · 4 minute read

Searching for a tutor often starts with a specific concern: homework takes too long, a pupil cannot explain their working, or a recent assessment has revealed a gap. Begin with that concern rather than choosing solely on the nearest postcode or the longest list of subjects. The right first conversation should help you understand what support is available and how progress will be checked.

Teaching Success offers a Smethwick tuition route and online options. You can meet Kevin and Serena and ask who would teach the relevant subject and level. Confirm the actual venue, time and availability before making travel plans.

Describe the difficulty with an example

Bring one recent piece of work, the pupil's year group and any teacher feedback. For GCSE or A-Level, include the subject, exam board and tier where relevant. “Needs help with maths” is a starting point; “can solve a simple equation but gets lost when there are brackets” is much easier to act on.

Ask the pupil what feels difficult too. They may describe a different problem from the one suggested by a mark: not understanding instructions, forgetting a method, rushing or struggling to begin. Avoid treating a single result as a complete account of their ability.

Compare the format as well as the tutor

FormatQuestions worth asking
One-to-oneHow will the lesson adapt when the pupil needs more explanation or is ready to move on?
Small groupHow many learners attend, how are they grouped, and how does each receive feedback?
OnlineHow will the pupil show working, ask questions and receive corrections?

No format is automatically best for every learner. A group can offer discussion and shared practice, while one-to-one teaching can focus closely on an individual gap. Online lessons can reduce travel but need a workable device, connection and way to share learning. Ask which formats are actually available rather than assuming every provider offers all three.

Use the first lesson to observe teaching

Look for explanation, pupil participation and a task attempted independently. A lesson can feel friendly without revealing whether the pupil understands the method. Ask what was noticed and what would happen next.

For example, after modelling a fraction problem, the tutor might change the numbers and ask the pupil to explain each step. If the pupil can only repeat the original answer, further teaching may be needed. This is more informative than simply counting completed worksheets.

Agree a short, reviewable goal

A useful goal is specific enough to check: “solve two-step equations and check by substitution” or “support a reading inference with evidence”. It need not be a promised grade increase. Ask when progress will be reviewed and what evidence will be used.

You might compare an independent task from the first lesson with a different task testing the same skill several weeks later. Make sure the difficulty is reasonably comparable. More completed work is not, by itself, proof of stronger understanding.

Ask practical questions before committing

  • Who will teach the lessons, and what experience is relevant to this subject?
  • What is the session length, full price and payment arrangement?
  • What happens if either side needs to cancel or change a lesson?
  • Is practice expected between sessions, and how much?
  • How are communication, safeguarding arrangements and collection or online contact handled?
  • How will the tutor coordinate with school learning without simply doing homework for the pupil?

Check claims about qualifications and experience directly with the provider. A website photograph or a promising headline is not a substitute for asking who will actually teach your child.

Make the next step manageable

Choose one priority and a lesson time the family can attend consistently. After the first session, ask the pupil to explain one thing they learned and one thing still unclear. Share this with the tutor so the next lesson responds to the evidence.

For a clearer record of maths difficulties, try the GCSE revision error log. For younger pupils considering entrance-test preparation, use the Year 5 weekly plan as a discussion tool, not a fixed timetable every child must follow.
