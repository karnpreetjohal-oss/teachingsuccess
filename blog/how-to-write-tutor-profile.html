How to write a tutor profile that helps parents understand your offer

Write a clear tutor profile with a practical structure, an illustrative example and checks for subjects, experience, lesson format and enquiries.

Tutor Launch business setup

By Teaching Success · Published 16 September 2026 · 4 minute read

A useful tutor profile helps a family decide whether your experience, subjects and approach fit their needs. It does not need inflated claims or a long autobiography. Start with the practical information a parent is trying to establish, then show how your teaching works through one concrete example.

Replace a broad promise with a visible lesson example

A tutor profile becomes more useful when a family can picture what the tutor actually does. “Personalised support” needs an example to distinguish it from an empty promise.

A practical example

An illustrative sentence might explain that a maths lesson begins with the learner’s own attempt, identifies the uncertain step and ends with a related independent question. Use this only if it accurately describes your practice; do not present an invented pupil story as a testimonial.

Check qualifications, subject limits, lesson formats and availability against your real offer. Remove guaranteed-grade language and unsupported claims about being the best. Ask a reader to name who the service suits after reading the profile once; if they cannot, make the scope clearer before adding more promotional adjectives.

Open with who and what you teach

In the first two sentences, identify the subjects and age groups you genuinely support. Add the lesson format and service area where relevant. “Online GCSE maths tuition for pupils who need help making sense of algebra” tells a family more than “passionate educator helping everyone achieve excellence”.

Be precise about the limits of your offer. Primary English, GCSE English Language and adult language teaching are different services. Do not list a subject or exam course simply because someone might search for it.

Explain experience without exaggerating

State relevant qualifications and experience accurately. Distinguish classroom teaching, tutoring, mentoring and subject knowledge rather than treating them as interchangeable. If you mention a qualification, use its correct name and be prepared to verify it.

Avoid claiming a number of years, pupils taught or grade improvements unless you have reliable records. A genuine description of how you teach is more useful than a statistic you cannot substantiate. Do not use another tutor's biography as your own.

Show what happens in a lesson

Describe a practical sequence: identify a starting point, explain a method, let the pupil practise and check an independent attempt. Explain how you respond when something is not understood. This helps a family imagine the lesson rather than guess what “personalised” means.

For example, you could describe changing the numbers in an algebra question after modelling the method, then asking the pupil to explain each step. Only describe a process you actually use or are ready to deliver.

Use a short example as a structure

The following is a fictional example, not a real tutor testimonial or a profile to copy word for word:

“I offer online GCSE maths support for pupils who want to strengthen their algebra and problem-solving. We begin by looking at a recent piece of work and identifying one priority. During lessons, I explain a method, work through an example with the pupil and check a different question independently. Families receive a clear next step for practice. Please tell me the pupil's year group, exam board and current difficulty when you enquire.”

Add your own verified background, availability and fee information. Replace the subject and approach with your actual service. A profile should sound like the person a parent will meet.

Make the next step straightforward

Tell readers how to enquire and what information is useful. Subject, year group, lesson format and broad availability are usually enough for an initial conversation. Avoid asking people to send sensitive pupil records or identity documents through a public profile or social-media comment.

Use a current photograph of yourself where possible. An illustrative or AI-generated image should not be presented as your own portrait or as a satisfied customer. Reviews must come from real experiences and be used with appropriate permission.

Read the profile as a parent

  • Can I quickly identify the subjects and levels offered?
  • Is it clear who will teach and what their relevant experience is?
  • Do I understand the lesson format and next step?
  • Are claims specific, accurate and capable of being checked?
  • Can I contact the tutor without searching through several pages?

Ask someone unfamiliar with your work to answer those questions after reading the profile. If they cannot, simplify the information before adding more detail. A website, social biography and enquiry response should describe the same offer consistently.
