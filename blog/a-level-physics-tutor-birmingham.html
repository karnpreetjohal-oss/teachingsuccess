What A-Level Physics should really do

A-Level Physics is rarely just about remembering facts. The pressure usually comes from linking equations, concepts, data and written reasoning without losing control of the method.

What A-Level Physics should include

  • Equation fluency, graph interpretation and required-practical thinking
  • Topic areas such as electricity, fields, mechanics and waves where earlier maths confidence matters
  • Paper practice that helps students decide when to calculate, explain or justify more carefully
  • A stronger bridge between Physics ideas and the mathematical method underneath them

Practical guide · Updated 23 September 2026

A gradient is only useful when the axes are understood

A straight-line graph can provide a physical constant, but only after the equation is matched to the quantities plotted. Rearranging before drawing avoids extracting the wrong value.

A practical example

For a spring within its linear elastic range, F = kx. Plotting force F vertically against extension x horizontally gives gradient k. Reversing the axes gives gradient 1/k. If extension is recorded in centimetres, converting to metres matters when quoting k in N/m.

Annotate the chosen equation with the vertical variable, horizontal variable and gradient before using data. State the range over which the model is being applied. A neat best-fit line cannot repair an incorrect interpretation of the axes or units.

A vector total needs directions as well as magnitudes

For perpendicular displacement components of 3 m east and 4 m north, the magnitude is 5 m, not 7 m. Draw the right-angled triangle and state the direction, approximately 53.1° north of east.

Contrast this with travelling 3 m east followed by 4 m east, where the displacement is 7 m east. The operation depends on direction, not simply the presence of two numbers. Ask the student to distinguish displacement from the distance travelled in the perpendicular journey, which is 7 m along that route.
